Linear Algebra for Homology

Kernels, images, quotients, and arithmetic over two elements

The focused linear algebra needed to define and compute homology.

Homology converts geometry into linear algebra. Cells become basis vectors, boundaries become matrices, and holes emerge from kernels and quotient spaces. This chapter develops only the algebra needed for that conversion.

NoteA geometric dictionary for the algebra

The chapter can be read with one picture in mind: take a square assembled from edges.

Linear-algebra term Topological meaning later
basis vector one cell, such as one edge
vector a selected collection of cells
linear map an operation such as taking the boundary
kernel chains whose boundary vanishes
image chains produced as boundaries of higher cells
quotient identify two cycles when they differ only by a filled boundary
dimension the number of independent features

The definitions below are general because the same algebra works in every dimension, but each has a concrete role in this table.

1 Fields and vector spaces

Definition 1 (Field and vector space) A field is a set in which addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division by nonzero elements behave as expected. Familiar examples are \(\mathbb Q\) and \(\mathbb R\).

For computation, we will often use

\[ \mathbb F_2=\{0,1\}, \]

with arithmetic modulo \(2\). In particular,

\[ 1+1=0. \]

A vector space \(V\) over a field \(\mathbb F\) is a set whose elements can be added and multiplied by scalars from \(\mathbb F\).

Over \(\mathbb F_2\), a vector is a binary string, vector addition is componentwise XOR, and every vector is its own additive inverse.

If a complex has ordered edges \((e_1,e_2,e_3,e_4)\), the binary vector

\[ (1,0,1,1) \]

means “select \(e_1,e_3,e_4\).” Adding \((1,1,0,0)\) gives \((0,1,1,1)\) because the repeated edge \(e_1\) cancels. This is why formal vector addition has a visible geometric interpretation.

2 Spans, independence, and bases

Given vectors \(v_1,\ldots,v_m\in V\), their span is

\[ \operatorname{span}\{v_1,\ldots,v_m\} = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^m a_iv_i:a_i\in\mathbb F \right\}. \]

The vectors are linearly independent if

\[ \sum_{i=1}^m a_iv_i=0 \]

forces every \(a_i=0\). A basis is a linearly independent spanning set. The number of elements in a finite basis is the dimension of the vector space.

Later, the \(k\)-cells of a complex will form a basis for a vector space \(C_k\). A chain is then a linear combination of cells.

3 Linear maps and matrices

A function \(T\colon V\to W\) is linear if

\[ T(av+bw)=aT(v)+bT(w) \]

for all vectors \(v,w\) and scalars \(a,b\).

After choosing bases, \(T\) is represented by a matrix \(A\). Its \(j\)th column is the coordinate vector of the image of the \(j\)th basis vector.

This convention matters for boundary matrices: columns correspond to cells in the domain, rows correspond to their facets in the codomain.

4 Kernel, image, and rank

The kernel of \(T\) is

\[ \ker T=\{v\in V:T(v)=0\}. \]

The image is

\[ \operatorname{im}T=\{T(v):v\in V\}. \]

Both are vector subspaces. Their dimensions are the nullity and rank of \(T\), respectively.

For finite-dimensional \(V\), the rank–nullity theorem states

\[ \dim V=\dim\ker T+\dim\operatorname{im}T. \]

This identity will turn the definition of homology into a practical formula for Betti numbers.

Theorem 1 (Rank–nullity theorem) Let \(T\colon V\to W\) be linear, where \(V\) is finite-dimensional. Then

\[ \dim V=\dim\ker T+\dim\operatorname{im}T. \]

Proof. Choose a basis \(v_1,\ldots,v_r\) of \(\ker T\) and extend it to a basis \(v_1,\ldots,v_r,v_{r+1},\ldots,v_n\) of \(V\). We claim that \(T(v_{r+1}),\ldots,T(v_n)\) is a basis for \(\operatorname{im}T\).

It spans the image because any \(v\in V\) is a linear combination of the \(v_i\), and the first \(r\) terms vanish under \(T\). For independence, if \(\sum_{i=r+1}^n a_iT(v_i)=0\), then \(\sum_{i=r+1}^n a_iv_i\in\ker T\), so it is also a combination of \(v_1,\ldots,v_r\). Uniqueness of coordinates in the full basis forces all \(a_i=0\). Hence \(\dim\operatorname{im}T=n-r\), and the formula follows.

5 Quotient spaces

Quotient spaces are usually the least familiar construction in this chapter. The guiding operation is declaring certain differences irrelevant. In clock arithmetic, for example, the hours \(2\) and \(14\) are treated as the same because their difference is a multiple of \(12\). In homology, two cycles are treated as the same when their difference is the boundary of a filled region.

Suppose \(W\) is a subspace of \(V\). Two vectors \(v,v'\in V\) are equivalent modulo \(W\) when

\[ v-v'\in W. \]

The equivalence class of \(v\) is the coset

\[ v+W=\{v+w:w\in W\}. \]

The set of all cosets is the quotient space \(V/W\).

Quotienting declares every vector in \(W\) to be algebraically trivial. This is exactly what homology needs: cycles that differ by the boundary of a higher-dimensional cell should represent the same hole.

Concretely, suppose \(z\) is the perimeter of a square and \(\kappa\) is its filled interior. Since \(\partial\kappa=z\), adding \(z\) to another cycle merely adds the boundary of a filled cell. In the quotient used by homology, the two cycles represent the same class. A quotient does not erase the vectors in the larger space; it groups vectors into equivalence classes according to the difference we have chosen to ignore.

If \(V\) is finite-dimensional, then

\[ \dim(V/W)=\dim V-\dim W. \]

Proposition 1 (Dimension of a quotient) If \(V\) is finite-dimensional and \(W\leq V\), then

\[ \dim(V/W)=\dim V-\dim W. \]

Proof. Choose a basis \(w_1,\ldots,w_r\) of \(W\) and extend it to a basis \(w_1,\ldots,w_r,v_{r+1},\ldots,v_n\) of \(V\). The cosets \(v_{r+1}+W,\ldots,v_n+W\) span \(V/W\) and are linearly independent there. They therefore form a basis of \(V/W\), which has dimension \(n-r\).

6 Why \(\mathbb F_2\) is convenient

Working over \(\mathbb F_2\) removes orientation signs. A cell is either present or absent from a chain. When the same face appears twice, it cancels:

\[ e+e=0. \]

For example, two adjacent squares share an edge. Adding their boundaries causes the shared edge to appear twice, so it vanishes. The resulting boundary is the outer perimeter.

This makes the theory and implementation clean. Other coefficient fields can detect information hidden by \(\mathbb F_2\), but binary coefficients are an excellent first model.

The thesis fixes \(\mathbb F_2\) for six related reasons:

  1. Orientation independence. Since \(-1=1\) in characteristic two, the alternating signs in an oriented boundary formula disappear.
  2. Binary incidence matrices. Boundary matrices contain only zeroes and ones and can be reduced using XOR.
  3. Chains as subsets. A cell has coefficient either zero or one, so a chain is identified with the subset of cells that occur.
  4. Transparent cancellation. Every codimension-two face in \(\partial^2\) occurs twice, and \(1+1=0\).
  5. Vector-space homology. Homology is characterised by dimensions, making Betti numbers directly accessible by rank–nullity.
  6. Gaussian elimination. Over a field, ordinary row reduction replaces the Smith normal form required for integral homology.

This is a choice of coefficients, not a theorem that \(\mathbb F_2\) is always best. Integral coefficients can detect torsion and retain orientation information. Those phenomena matter in many topological spaces, but not for the thesis’s narrow task of counting components and planar loops in digital images. The notation \(\mathbb F_2\) is used rather than \(\mathbb Z_2\) to emphasise the field structure and avoid confusion with the ring of \(2\)-adic integers.

7 Gaussian elimination over \(\mathbb F_2\)

Ranks and kernels are computed by row reduction. The legal operations are:

  1. swap two rows;
  2. add one row to another; and
  3. omit rescaling, because the only nonzero scalar is \(1\).

Addition is XOR, making binary elimination especially efficient.

Consider

\[ A= \begin{pmatrix} 1&1&0\\ 1&0&1\\ 0&1&1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Over \(\mathbb R\) this matrix has rank \(3\). Over \(\mathbb F_2\), the third row is the sum of the first two, so its rank is \(2\). Coefficients matter.

8 Direct sums

This short section is a look ahead. Direct sums become important only when a persistence module is decomposed into independent feature lifespans.

The direct sum \(V\oplus W\) consists of pairs \((v,w)\) with componentwise operations. Dimensions add:

\[ \dim(V\oplus W)=\dim V+\dim W. \]

Persistent homology uses direct sums to decompose a complicated evolving vector space into independent interval modules, one for each birth–death interval.

9 The algebraic destination

For boundary maps

\[ C_{k+1}\xrightarrow{\partial_{k+1}} C_k\xrightarrow{\partial_k}C_{k-1}, \]

the identity \(\partial_k\partial_{k+1}=0\) implies

\[ \operatorname{im}\partial_{k+1} \subseteq \ker\partial_k. \]

Therefore, the quotient

\[ H_k= \ker\partial_k/ \operatorname{im}\partial_{k+1} \]

is well-defined. Its dimension is the \(k\)th Betti number.

TipWhat to carry forward

The expression

\[ H_k=\ker\partial_k/\operatorname{im}\partial_{k+1} \]

says: begin with every \(k\)-dimensional chain whose boundary vanishes, then identify chains that differ by the boundary of a filled \((k+1)\)-dimensional chain. The next chapter constructs these two spaces geometrically. If quotient notation still feels unfamiliar, this verbal reading is sufficient to proceed.

10 Exercises

  1. List all vectors in \(\mathbb F_2^3\).
  2. Find a basis for the span of \((1,1,0)\) and \((0,1,1)\) over \(\mathbb F_2\).
  3. Row-reduce the matrix \(A\) above over \(\mathbb F_2\).
  4. If \(\dim V=8\) and \(\dim W=3\), compute \(\dim(V/W)\).
  5. Explain geometrically why shared edges cancel when adding two square boundaries over \(\mathbb F_2\).
