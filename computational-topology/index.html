Topological Data Analysis from Scratch

A computational algebraic topology route from metric spaces to persistent homology

These notes develop the mathematical foundations of topological data analysis (TDA) through computational algebraic topology. Starting from distance and continuity, we build towards cubical homology, persistence, stability, and image-based applications. They are intended for readers with some mathematical maturity, but no prior knowledge of topology.

Work in progress. Every planned chapter currently has a draft, but this TDA section is still under development. The exposition, notation, proofs, examples, exercises, and implementations may change as the notes are revised.

ImportantBachelor’s thesis and repository

The contents of these notes are based on my BSc Mathematics thesis, Computational Stability of Cubical Homology: Orthogonal Controls for Fault Isolation in Retinal Diagnostics. The public thesis and its accompanying code and materials are available below.

Read the bachelor’s thesis Open the project repository

I have adapted and reorganised the thesis into this developing set of notes so that I can expand the exposition gradually. A thesis has strict length constraints, which limited how fully I could motivate definitions, develop examples, and explain proofs. Here, I intend to fill those gaps and make the material accessible to readers whose mathematical background consists of basic calculus.

The presentation is organised as a course: definitions are introduced before they are used, proofs are separated from implementation, and the retinal application appears only after the necessary mathematical foundations.

The central idea is to turn geometric and topological questions into finite algebraic computations. In particular, we will study why

\[ \partial_k \circ \partial_{k+1}=0 \]

and how this identity leads to homology groups, algorithms, filtrations, and persistent homology.

TipHow to read these notes

The chapters are cumulative, but they are not meant to be read at a uniform speed. A first encounter with metric spaces, quotient spaces, or functors can feel much more abstract than the image-processing problem that motivated them. When that happens, keep the following concrete dictionary in view:

Abstract object Picture to keep in mind
metric a rule for deciding how different two points or images are
open neighbourhood the points allowed by a chosen tolerance
complex pixels together with their edges and vertices
chain a selected collection of cells
boundary map “keep only the perimeter”
homology class a loop or component after filled-in boundaries are ignored
filtration a thresholded image growing one level at a time
persistence interval the lifespan of one topological feature

On a first reading, concentrate on the examples and the statements of the results. Proofs marked as sketches and the final chapter on category theory can be revisited after the computational pipeline is familiar. None of the applications requires the reader to become fluent in categorical notation immediately.

The route through the subject

The course repeatedly moves through the same four levels:

\[ \text{distance and continuity} \longrightarrow \text{finite shapes} \longrightarrow \text{linear algebra} \longrightarrow \text{stable image descriptors}. \]

Metric and topological spaces provide the language of closeness and deformation. Graphs and cell complexes replace continuous shapes by finite objects. Linear algebra turns their boundaries into matrices. Persistent homology then follows the resulting features through an image filtration, while stability theory tells us how sensitive those features are to changes in the data.

Why turn topology into vectors?

Neural networks and most other statistical learning methods ultimately operate on numerical inputs. A digital image is already a numerical array, and the usual neural-network architecture for image data is a convolutional neural network (CNN). A CNN repeatedly transforms pixel arrays into learned numerical features. We will not develop that machinery here.

Convolutional networks are effective at learning local visual patterns and can, in principle, learn information related to shape and topology. Standard CNN architectures, however, do not explicitly represent connected components, loops, or voids, nor do they ordinarily guarantee that these quantities are preserved through their learned transformations. When those structures matter, it is useful to provide them to a learning system directly rather than require the network to rediscover them from pixels.

The pipeline developed in these notes therefore has the form

\[ \text{digital image} \longrightarrow \text{cubical filtration} \longrightarrow \text{persistence diagrams} \longrightarrow \text{topological vector} \longrightarrow \text{statistical or neural model}. \]

The topological vector is a fixed-length numerical summary of components, loops, and their persistence across thresholds. It can be concatenated with ordinary image or geometric features, used as an interpretable auxiliary input, or studied independently with a simple classifier.

Producing numbers is not enough: the conversion must also be stable. If two images differ only by a minute, physically insignificant perturbation, we would like their topological vectors to remain close. In schematic form, the desired guarantee is

\[ \text{small image change} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{small topological-vector change}. \]

Establishing when this implication holds—and identifying when sampling, interpolation, or an unstable vectorisation can break it—is one of the main purposes of the metric, persistence, and stability chapters.

NoteProof-oriented presentation

These notes follow the convention of a typical undergraduate mathematics course: definitions fix the objects under discussion; lemmas isolate useful steps; propositions and theorems state the main conclusions; and proofs make the logical dependence explicit. Results proved in the thesis source are included here whenever they fit the level and scope of the chapter. A proof sketch is labelled as such when a complete proof requires machinery developed elsewhere.

NoteWhy the development is cubical

Many introductions to computational topology develop the subject primarily through simplicial complexes. This is the standard and more flexible language: simplices can model arbitrary meshes and combinatorial spaces without requiring an underlying rectangular grid.

These notes instead develop the main constructions first for cubical complexes. This is a deliberate choice motivated by the application. Digital images are already made of pixels, and volumetric images are made of voxels; these are naturally \(2\)- and \(3\)-dimensional cubes. A cubical model therefore preserves the native geometry and adjacency structure of the data, without first subdividing every pixel or voxel into simplices.

The two viewpoints are not competing theories. Cubical complexes can be triangulated, and the later chapter on functoriality and triangulation explains how the Coxeter–Freudenthal–Kuhn construction passes from cubes to simplices while preserving the relevant chain and homology structure. Thus the cubical development remains connected to the broader simplicial theory, while keeping the image-based motivation visible from the outset.
