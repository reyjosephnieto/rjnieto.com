Exorcising a Demon IV: The NHS, Wimbledon, Bristol, and Filipino Psychology

What kapwa can tell us about Alex Eala, a Filipino student in Bristol, and the care offered by Filipino NHS nurses.
Author

Rey Joseph Nieto

Published

5 August 2026

← Previous post: Exorcising a Demon III: From Bristol to Bristol

Before explaining just how wrong I was about my final-year project, I need to take a detour. Rereading the first three parts of this series, I noticed that I had moved from paranoid defeatism to cautious optimism without losing the habit of making jokes at my own expense. The little bit of Britain I had acquired thought, good grief, this sounds like a soap opera. A restrained Englishman might have said that university had been “a bit difficult” and left it there.

Perhaps there is some melodrama in them, but the Filipino heart was doing most of the talking. My family, friends and lecturers were not supporting characters in an individual story; they were part of how I understood myself. Perhaps it was only then that I began to see the people at Bristol as kapwa, rather than as potential judges.

Filipino Nurses and a Tennis Player

That is why this detour begins in an NHS hospital ward. Most of us will need a nurse eventually, and Britain has a horde of Filipino nurses. One of them may well be caring for you. Their care may feel unusually personal: more patient than the circumstances reasonably permit or more concerned about your comfort than the job strictly requires.

This is not a theorem about Filipino nurses. A population of more than a hundred million people will provide counterexamples to almost any proposition. I am interested in the narrower question of why some Filipino nurses appear to treat care as something more personal than a professional transaction.

To understand that, consider Alex Eala. Her ranking bears her name, and she wins or loses her own matches. Yet Filipinos fill courts wherever she plays. They queued for hours at Wimbledon and followed her to Washington, where she won her first tour-level WTA title. The Philippines, which is not known as a tennis country, promptly developed several million experts on second serves.

After her Wimbledon run, President Ferdinand Marcos Jr received her at Malacañan Palace and praised the way she carried her Filipino identity onto the court. Eala has said much the same thing herself: where she comes from forms part of who she is. She competes on an individual professional tour, yet Filipinos experience her victories as partly theirs.

The Audience in My Head

I understand this because something similar happened inside my head at Bristol. There was no flag beside my name on an examination paper, but I worried that my performance reflected upon Filipinos generally. If I struggled, I felt that I was confirming somebody’s doubts about us; if I did well, I felt that I had defended a national reputation nobody had asked me to defend.

My lecturers had imposed none of this. They could distinguish one Filipino with a chequered academic history from an entire country. If I failed Linear Algebra, nobody was going to summon the Philippine ambassador. The audience was largely imaginary, but that did not make it harmless.

Virgilio Enriquez’s concept of kapwa helps explain it. Enriquez, the founder of Sikolohiyang Pilipino, argued that Filipino psychology should begin with Filipino language and experience rather than assume that concepts developed elsewhere would fit us perfectly.

Kapwa is sometimes translated as “other person”, although that loses much of its meaning. In English, the other is separate from me. With kapwa, the other person and I share something of the same identity. This does not mean Filipinos live in perpetual harmony; anyone who has attended a Filipino family gathering will know better. It means that the boundary between what belongs to me and what belongs to others is not always absolute. For better or worse, kapwa forms part of our cultural firmware.

I had encountered this when I left content creation. I had around two million followers, most of whom were Filipino, and many felt betrayed when I left public life to return to university. They did not want me to remain stupid; they had simply come to regard my political work as something we shared, so my departure did not appear to be an entirely private decision.

Looking back, I suspect they understood my departure through kapwa. I was trying to finish the education I had abandoned; they saw me walking away from something in which they had also invested. I cannot blame them, but leaving was something I had to do. The work bore my name without belonging only to me.

Back to the Ward

The same idea may help us understand your Filipino NHS nurse. I am not a nurse, and using retinal images in a mathematics project does not qualify me to speak for one. I suspect, however, that kapwa explains part of what patients sometimes experience as unusually personal care.

A patient may begin as a stranger but remains kapwa: another person whose suffering is not entirely separate from one’s own. There is also pakikiramdam, the effort to notice what another person feels without waiting for a formal request. The nurse is unlikely to think, “I shall now demonstrate Filipino culture to Bed 12.” They may simply find it difficult to walk away while somebody remains frightened or uncomfortable.

Professional training, experience and temperament still matter. Kapwa is not a complete explanation, but it helps explain why care may feel personal even when the relationship began as a professional one.

There is also a danger. Describing Filipinos as naturally caring, selfless and hardworking gives an overstretched health service a convenient excuse for expecting too much from them. A study of Filipino nurses in Britain noted that, during the first two months of the COVID-19 pandemic, Filipinos were estimated to account for 36 per cent of known healthcare-worker deaths despite comprising about 8 per cent of the NHS nursing workforce. Being good at caring for other people does not make somebody indestructible.

Eala should be allowed to lose without feeling that she has failed the Philippines. I should have been able to perform badly in an examination without treating it as an international incident. A Filipino nurse should be able to finish a shift without believing that exhaustion represents a failure to care.

Kapwa is not the demon that needs exorcising. It fills tennis courts for a young woman from Quezon City, helped the son of a pig farmer and grandson of a rice farmer survive at a university half a planet away, and can make a frightened patient feel that somebody sees them. The demon is the belief that belonging must always be earned through achievement and sacrifice.

If a Filipino nurse cares for you, say thank you, but do not mistake that kindness for an unlimited resource. Kapwa may help explain why they care; it does not mean they do not need caring for.

As for my final-year project, that particular disaster will have to wait.
