Exorcising a Demon I: From Manila to London

Returning to university after nearly two decades and finding a route back to mathematics.
Author

Rey Joseph Nieto

Published

2 August 2026

“He who has a why to live for can bear almost any how.” — Friedrich Nietzsche

Four years ago, I decided to leave public life at what was probably the height of my career. After nearly a decade as a professional content creator, I felt it was time to move on and pursue other interests.

I had spent years in the political firing line, taking figurative bullets for what I believed was right—whether I was right or mistaken. I was also getting older, and it had become clear that I needed to start looking after myself because no one else was going to do it for me.

Fiery opinion writing was never on my bingo card when I was younger. Fresh out of secondary school, I enrolled at the University of the Philippines Diliman to study mathematics. Things went well for a time—I believe I was among the strongest students in my class—until life intervened. Suffice it to say that the financial difficulties my family faced after my father’s death set off a chain of events that eventually forced me to leave university at the age of eighteen.

I was something of a lost soul for much of my twenties, and it took me a long time to recover. The recovery was never complete, but life gradually became more manageable. For better or worse, I eventually found myself in the political arena, where I remained for eight years.

Two presidential elections later, however, I realised that I was getting older and that an old inner demon still needed exorcising.

In a culture that places an almost sacred value on education, the fact that I had never completed my degree haunted me endlessly. At thirty-seven, I decided it was time to take another shot at it and, once and for all, lay that ghost to rest. I needed to finish my mathematics degree.

In my mind, returning to a Philippine university was no longer an option. I assumed that my chequered academic record, together with the public profile created by my subsequent political career, would make me an impossible prospect for any admissions panel. I wanted to begin again abroad, far from the political noise that had followed me for years.

So I looked overseas.

I knew that my circumstances were unusual and that my academic credentials were nearly two decades old. I applied to foreign universities with the private understanding that the whole exercise was a moonshot. I must have applied to more than a hundred institutions. After more than a hundred rejection letters, four eventually offered me a place.

The first was for Finance and Investment at the University of Kent in south-east England. The second was for Applied Mathematics and Statistics at SUNY Korea in Incheon. The third was for Commerce at the University of Canterbury in Christchurch, New Zealand. The fourth was for Economics at the University of York in northern England.

Of the four, York felt like the best fit for me, so I accepted its offer.

There was, however, a catch. Before beginning my first year, I had to complete a year-long international foundation programme. My academic qualifications were simply too old to be accepted at face value, and York wanted proof that I was ready to return to formal study.

I looked into my options and found what seemed like a shortcut: Kaplan International College London offered an intensive six-month foundation programme that could lead to York.

So I took it. I did not care that I would end up studying economics rather than mathematics. I was simply grateful to have been given a chance at a new life.

My first day at college in London brought its own private humiliation. I was in my late thirties, while most of my classmates were quite literally two decades younger than I was. They could have been my own children. I was even older than some of my teachers. No one treated me differently, but I still found it embarrassing to walk into the classroom each day, feeling that I had entered a place meant for the young, not merely the young at heart.

Still, I was paying for the whole thing with money I had earned from content creation. I had no choice but to swallow my pride and study as hard as I could.

By the third month, after the midterm examinations, I was among the top students in my class. Mathematics had made the difference. It had always been my first love, and it turned out that, despite everything, it had not entirely forgotten me.

While considering what to do next, I discovered that Kaplan London also sent students to the University of Bristol. More importantly, Bristol offered the subject I had wanted to return to all along: mathematics.

My confidence, however, had not recovered as quickly as my ambition. I was not yet prepared to stake everything on a degree in mathematics alone, so I chose Mathematics and Economics instead. It was partly a hedge: economics would still involve considerable abstraction and quantitative work, but I hoped that the broader combination might be a little kinder to me if mathematics proved overwhelming.

I applied to change pathways, and the college agreed—but on the condition that my required progression mark be raised.

It was a considerable risk. If I failed, I would return to Manila not merely as someone who had dropped out of university once, but as someone who had done so both in the Philippines and abroad.
