The Language of Mathematics

Mathematical Language and Symbols

Mathematics can appear daunting because it is often presented using unfamiliar symbols. To a new reader, a line of notation may look like a sentence written in an alien language. Mathematical symbols, however, are not intended to hide ideas. They allow us to express ideas that would otherwise require many words.

Like every language, mathematics has vocabulary, grammar, and conventions. Its vocabulary consists of symbols and specialised terms. Its grammar tells us which arrangements of symbols are meaningful. Its conventions allow different readers to interpret the same notation consistently.

Mathematical language is especially valued because it is:

  • precise: it allows distinctions that ordinary language may leave ambiguous;
  • concise: it can communicate an idea using relatively few symbols; and
  • powerful: it can describe complicated structures and relationships in a form that can be analysed logically.

These advantages do not mean that mathematical writing should consist only of symbols. Good mathematical communication combines notation with clear prose. The symbols compress the ideas; the surrounding words tell the reader what those ideas mean and why they matter.

NoteLearning outcomes

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:

  • distinguish mathematical expressions, open sentences, and closed sentences;
  • translate elementary statements between words and symbols;
  • use common mathematical symbols and conventions correctly;
  • apply the order-of-operations convention without treating PEMDAS mechanically;
  • explain the elementary meanings of sets, relations, functions, and binary operations;
  • interpret elementary logical connectives and quantifiers;
  • distinguish a statement from its converse and its negation;
  • explain how formality reduces ambiguity in mathematical communication; and
  • recognise examples of these concepts in Biology and everyday life.

Expressions and sentences

The distinction between an expression and a sentence is similar to the distinction between a noun phrase and a complete sentence in English.

NoteDefinition: mathematical expression

A mathematical expression is a well-formed arrangement of symbols that represents a mathematical object, value, or quantity. An expression does not, by itself, make a claim that can be judged true or false.

NoteDefinition: mathematical sentence

A mathematical sentence is a well-formed arrangement of symbols that makes a mathematical assertion. A closed sentence, also called a proposition, has a definite truth value: it is either true or false.

An assertion whose truth depends on an unspecified variable is commonly called an open sentence. Assigning the variable a value—or binding it with a phrase such as “for every” or “there exists”—can turn it into a closed sentence.

TipExamples: expressions and sentences
Notation Classification Reason
\(b^2-4ac\) expression represents the discriminant of \(ax^2+bx+c\) but makes no assertion
\(m/V\) expression may represent density but makes no assertion
\(x+1=2\) open sentence true only for particular values of \(x\)
\(1+1=2\) true closed sentence has a definite truth value
\(1+1=3\) false closed sentence has a definite truth value
\(\forall x\in\mathbb R,\ x^2\geq0\) true closed sentence the variable is bound by “for every”

Sentences often join expressions using relations such as \(=,\neq,<,\leq,\) and \(\in\). The equality sign in \(1+1=2\), for example, asserts that the expressions on its two sides represent the same number.

Reading and writing mathematical symbols

Symbols should be read as meaningful words or phrases, not as decorative marks. The following are some common examples.

\(xy\) the product of \(x\) and \(y\)
\(\dfrac{x}{y}\) \(x\) divided by \(y\)
\(x=y\) \(x\) is equal to \(y\)
\(x\neq y\) \(x\) is not equal to \(y\)
\(x<y\) \(x\) is less than \(y\)
\(x\leq y\) \(x\) is less than or equal to \(y\)
\(x\in A\) \(x\) is an element of \(A\)
\(A\subseteq B\) \(A\) is a subset of \(B\)
\(\forall x\) for every \(x\)
\(\exists x\) there exists an \(x\)
\(P\Rightarrow Q\) if \(P\) is true, then \(Q\) is true
\(P\Leftrightarrow Q\) \(P\) is true if and only if \(Q\) is true

Translating between words and symbols

TipExample: translating a temperature condition

Consider the sentence:

The body temperature \(T\) is at least \(38^\circ\text{C}\).

The phrase at least includes \(38\), so it can be written as

\[ T\geq38. \]

The notation \(T\geq38\) illustrates the three qualities introduced at the beginning of this chapter:

  • It is concise because it expresses the condition on body temperature using only a few symbols.
  • It is precise once we have established that \(T\) denotes temperature measured in degrees Celsius and that \(\geq\) means “is greater than or equal to.” By contrast, “the body temperature is greater than \(38^\circ\text{C}\)” becomes \(T>38\); removing the small bar excludes a temperature of exactly \(38^\circ\text{C}\).
  • It is powerful because it places the English sentence in a form that can readily be incorporated into calculations, compared with other inequalities, or manipulated using the rules of algebra.
Additional translation examples

Here are further examples.

Words Symbols
\(x\) is positive \(x>0\)
\(n\) is no more than 20 \(n\leq20\)
\(p\) is between 0 and 1, inclusive \(0\leq p\leq1\)
\(x\) belongs to the set \(A\) \(x\in A\)
\(A\) and \(B\) have no common elements \(A\cap B=\varnothing\)
there is a real number whose square is 4 \(\exists x\in\mathbb R\colon x^2=4\)

Translation requires attention to meaning rather than word substitution. For example, “less than” reverses the order in which quantities may appear: “five less than \(x\)” is \(x-5\), not \(5-x\).

Conventions in mathematical language

Mathematical conventions are agreed ways of writing and interpreting mathematics. Without them, two readers could assign different meanings to the same line of notation.

Order of operations

One familiar convention is the order of operations, often remembered using PEMDAS:

  1. parentheses;
  2. exponents;
  3. multiplication and division; and
  4. addition and subtraction.
TipExample: applying the order of operations

\[ \begin{aligned} 1+2-3(4+5) &=1+2-3(9) &&\text{parentheses}\\ &=1+2-27 &&\text{multiplication}\\ &=3-27 &&\text{addition from left to right}\\ &=-24. &&\text{subtraction} \end{aligned} \]

If we ignored the convention and performed the subtraction before the multiplication, we would obtain a different result.

WarningPEMDAS is not simply a six-step ladder

Multiplication and division have the same precedence and are evaluated from left to right. Addition and subtraction also have the same precedence and are evaluated from left to right.

Thus,

\[ 18\div3\times2=6\times2=12, \]

not \(3\). The letters M and D in PEMDAS do not mean that every multiplication must be performed before every division.

Other common conventions

  • If \(n\) is a positive integer, then \(x^n=\underbrace{x\cdot x\cdots x}_{n\text{ factors}}\).
  • Symbols written next to one another usually indicate multiplication: \(3x=3\cdot x\) and \(ab=a\cdot b\).
  • A fraction bar groups the entire numerator and denominator: \(\dfrac{a+b}{c+d}\) means \((a+b)\div(c+d)\).
  • Parentheses can change the object to which an operation applies: \(-3^2=-(3^2)=-9\), whereas \((-3)^2=9\).
  • The notation \(f(x)\) usually means the value of the function \(f\) at \(x\); it does not ordinarily mean \(f\) multiplied by \(x\).
  • The equality sign expresses sameness of value. Every expression in a chain such as \(a=b=c\) must represent the same object.
  • The approximation sign \(\approx\) should be used when quantities are close but not exactly equal. For example, \(\pi\approx3.14\), not \(\pi=3.14\).

Conventions are not laws of nature. They are shared agreements that make mathematical communication efficient and consistent.

Four basic concepts

Four ideas appear repeatedly across mathematics: sets, relations, functions, and binary operations. They provide a useful vocabulary for describing collections of objects and the ways those objects are connected or combined.

Sets

NoteDefinition: set

For the purposes of this course, a set is a well-defined collection of distinct objects. The objects belonging to the set are called its elements.

TipExample: set notation

Sets are usually denoted by capital letters and their elements by lowercase letters. If

\[ A=\{\text{adenine},\text{cytosine},\text{guanine},\text{thymine}\}, \]

then \(\text{adenine}\in A\), meaning that adenine is an element of \(A\).

Repeated elements are not counted more than once, and the order in which elements are listed does not matter:

\[ \{1,2,2,3\}=\{3,2,1\}=\{1,2,3\}. \]

If every element of \(A\) is also an element of \(B\), we write \(A\subseteq B\) and say that \(A\) is a subset of \(B\). The set with no elements is the empty set, denoted by \(\varnothing\).

Important operations on sets include

\[ A\cup B \quad\text{(union)},\qquad A\cap B \quad\text{(intersection)}. \]

The union contains elements that belong to \(A\), to \(B\), or to both. The intersection contains only the elements common to both sets.

Why this is called a naïve definition — non-examinable

The definition above belongs to naïve set theory and is sufficient for this course. In more advanced mathematics, allowing absolutely any verbal description to define a set creates contradictions.

Russell’s paradox considers the supposed set

\[ R=\{X:X\notin X\}, \]

the collection of all sets that are not elements of themselves. If \(R\in R\), then its defining property says \(R\notin R\). But if \(R\notin R\), then it satisfies the defining property and therefore \(R\in R\). Modern axiomatic set theory carefully restricts how sets may be constructed to avoid this contradiction. For now, treat this as an interesting piece of mathematical trivia: the foundations of axiomatic set theory lie well beyond the scope of this course.

Ordered pairs and Cartesian products

TipExample: ordered pairs and the Cartesian plane

An ordered pair \((a,b)\) records both its entries and their order. In general, \((a,b)\neq(b,a)\). The Cartesian product

\[ A\times B=\{(a,b):a\in A\text{ and }b\in B\}. \]

is simply the set of all possible pairs with \(a\in A\) and \(b\in B\). The familiar Cartesian plane is

\[ \mathbb R\times\mathbb R=\mathbb R^2. \]

Each point \((x,y)\) represents one element: \(x\) gives its horizontal position and \(y\) its vertical position.

Relations

NoteDefinition: relation

A relation from \(A\) to \(B\) is a subset of \(A\times B\). It records which elements of \(A\) are related to which elements of \(B\).

Relations may also be defined on a single set. In an ecological network, for example, “preys upon” is a relation on a set of species.

TipExample: a relation as a dietary model

Let

\[ A=\{\text{dog},\text{wolf},\text{hyena}\}, \qquad B=\{\text{carnivore},\text{herbivore}\}. \]

In a deliberately simplified model, allow the two dietary labels to overlap. The resulting relation might be

\[ R= \{ (\text{dog},\text{carnivore}), (\text{dog},\text{herbivore}), (\text{wolf},\text{carnivore}), (\text{hyena},\text{carnivore}) \}. \]

The dog has two outputs, so \(R\) is not a function from \(A\) to \(B\). To remove that ambiguity, introduce the mutually exclusive category “omnivore”:

\[ B'=\{\text{carnivore},\text{herbivore},\text{omnivore}\}. \]

Define \(f\colon A\to B'\) by

\[ f(\text{dog})=\text{omnivore},\qquad f(\text{wolf})=\text{carnivore},\qquad f(\text{hyena})=\text{carnivore}. \]

The revised rule assigns every animal exactly one output and is therefore a function. It is clearer for this task, although actual diets remain more complicated. As Chapter 1 emphasised, models should be refined when their simplifications become misleading.

Functions

NoteDefinition: function

A function \(f\) from a set \(A\) to a set \(B\), written \(f\colon A\to B\), is a relation that assigns every element of \(A\) to exactly one element of \(B\).

The set \(A\) is the domain, and \(B\) is the codomain. The set of outputs that the function actually produces is its range, which is a subset of the codomain.

The phrase “exactly one” is essential. Different inputs may have the same output, but one input cannot be assigned two different outputs by the same function.

TipExample: DNA base pairing as a function

Let

\[ D=\{A,C,G,T\} \]

be the set of DNA bases, where \(A\) denotes adenine, \(C\) denotes cytosine, \(G\) denotes guanine, and \(T\) denotes thymine. These four bases form part of the familiar double-helix structure shown below.

You may recall from high-school Biology that the two strands of DNA are held together by specific complementary base pairing: adenine pairs with thymine, while cytosine pairs with guanine. This pairing rule defines a function \(f\colon D\to D\) by

\[ f(A)=T,\qquad f(T)=A,\qquad f(C)=G,\qquad f(G)=C. \]

Every input base has exactly one complementary base, so this rule is a function.

WarningA common incomplete definition

It is not enough to say only that a function never assigns two different outputs to the same input. A function \(f\colon A\to B\) must also assign an output to every element of its stated domain \(A\).

Binary operations

NoteDefinition: binary operation

A binary operation on a set \(A\) is a function

\[ \star\colon A\times A\to A. \]

It takes two elements of \(A\), combines them according to a rule, and produces another element of \(A\).

TipExamples: binary operations and closure

Ordinary addition is a binary operation on the integers because the sum of any two integers is another integer:

\[ +\colon\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z. \]

Union is a binary operation on a collection of sets because the union of two sets is again a set in that collection, provided the collection has been chosen appropriately.

The choice of the underlying set matters. Subtraction is not a binary operation on the positive integers because, for example, \(2-3=-1\) is not a positive integer. Division is not a binary operation on all real numbers because division by zero is undefined.

TipExample: a custom binary operation

The symbol used for an operation acquires its meaning from its definition. It need not represent one of the familiar operations. For example, define a new operation \(\star\) on the real numbers by

\[ a\star b=a+2b. \]

We then calculate by applying the stated rule:

\[ 3\star4=3+2(4)=11. \]

Further example: associativity — non-examinable

Nested operations must be evaluated carefully:

\[ (3\star4)\star2=11\star2=11+2(2)=15, \]

whereas

\[ 3\star(4\star2)=3\star8=3+2(8)=19. \]

Thus, this particular operation is not associative. The example demonstrates why we must read the definition of an unfamiliar symbol rather than assume that it behaves like ordinary addition or multiplication.

Elementary logic

Mathematical language is governed not only by conventions but also by logic, the study of correct reasoning. Logic helps us combine statements and determine what conclusions follow from them.

NoteScope of this section

The intention here is to give you a working familiarity with elementary logic, not to provide a full course in symbolic or mathematical logic. You should learn to read the basic logical connectives and quantifiers, interpret simple logical statements, complete a basic two-variable truth table, and distinguish a conditional statement from its converse.

Complicated truth tables involving many nested statements, formal proofs of logical equivalence, and more advanced systems of logic lie beyond the scope of this course unless explicitly stated otherwise.

NoteDefinition: proposition

A proposition is a closed sentence that is either true or false. It cannot be both true and false under the same interpretation.

TipExample: proposition versus non-proposition

“A dog is a mammal” is a proposition. The expression \(x+1\) is not a proposition because it makes no assertion, while the open sentence \(x+1=2\) does not have a definite truth value until \(x\) is specified or quantified.

Logical connectives

Simple propositions can be joined to form compound propositions. Let \(P\) and \(Q\) denote propositions.

Symbol Name Reading When it is true
\(\neg P\) negation not \(P\) when \(P\) is false
\(P\land Q\) conjunction \(P\) and \(Q\) when both are true
\(P\lor Q\) disjunction \(P\) or \(Q\) when at least one is true
\(P\Rightarrow Q\) conditional if \(P\), then \(Q\) except when \(P\) is true and \(Q\) is false
\(P\Leftrightarrow Q\) biconditional \(P\) if and only if \(Q\) when \(P\) and \(Q\) have the same truth value

In mathematics, or is normally inclusive: \(P\lor Q\) is true when \(P\) is true, when \(Q\) is true, or when both are true.

TipExample: a truth table for the logical connectives

A truth table displays the truth value of a compound proposition for every possible combination of truth values of its parts.

\(P\) \(Q\) \(\neg P\) \(P\land Q\) \(P\lor Q\) \(P\Rightarrow Q\) \(P\Leftrightarrow Q\)
T T F T T T T
T F F F T F F
F T T F T T F
F F T F F T T
TipExample: a conditional and its converse

The conditional deserves particular attention. Let

  • \(P\) mean “the organism is a dog”; and
  • \(Q\) mean “the organism is a mammal.”

Then \(P\Rightarrow Q\) says, “If the organism is a dog, then it is a mammal.” Under the usual biological classification, this is true. Its converse is \(Q\Rightarrow P\): “If the organism is a mammal, then it is a dog.” The converse is false because many mammals are not dogs.

WarningImplication is not causation

The notation \(P\Rightarrow Q\) means that whenever \(P\) is true, \(Q\) must also be true. It does not by itself say that \(P\) physically causes \(Q\), nor does it say that the converse \(Q\Rightarrow P\) is true.

Quantifiers

Quantifiers tell us how many elements satisfy an open sentence.

Symbol Reading
\(\forall\) for every, or for all
\(\exists\) there exists at least one
\(\exists!\) there exists exactly one
TipExample: quantifying DNA base pairing

The DNA complementary-base function satisfies

\[ \forall x\in D,\qquad f(f(x))=x. \]

This says that, for every DNA base \(x\) in \(D\), taking the complement twice returns the original base.

TipExample: why the domain matters

The domain of a quantifier matters. The sentence

\[ \forall x\in\mathbb Z,\quad \exists y\in\mathbb Z\text{ such that }x+y=0 \]

is true. Given any integer \(x\), we may choose \(y=-x\), which is also an integer. The number \(y\) is called the additive inverse of \(x\).

By contrast, the sentence

\[ \forall x\in\mathbb N,\quad \exists y\in\mathbb N\text{ such that }x+y=0 \]

is false when \(\mathbb N\) denotes the natural or counting numbers. For example, if \(x=1\), the only real number satisfying \(1+y=0\) is \(y=-1\), and \(-1\notin\mathbb N\). The same equation therefore produces different truth values when the permitted domain changes.

Biological extension: classification depends on the domain — non-examinable

The same issue arises in biological classification. Consider the traditional five-kingdom model, and let \(\mathcal L\) be the set of living cellular organisms covered by that model. Let

\[ \mathcal K= \{ \text{Monera}, \text{Protista}, \text{Fungi}, \text{Plantae}, \text{Animalia} \}, \]

where each kingdom is itself regarded as a set of organisms. We can express the claim that every organism receives a kingdom classification as

\[ \forall x\in\mathcal L,\quad \exists Y\in\mathcal K\text{ such that }x\in Y. \]

Now suppose we broaden the domain to a set \(\mathcal G\) containing all biological entities that possess a genome, while leaving \(\mathcal K\) unchanged. The corresponding claim

\[ \forall x\in\mathcal G,\quad \exists Y\in\mathcal K\text{ such that }x\in Y \]

is false because the enlarged domain includes viruses, which are not assigned to any of the five kingdoms. The varicella-zoster virus—the virus that causes chickenpox and shingles—is one counterexample: it has a double-stranded DNA genome, but it does not belong to Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, or Animalia. Defining the domain as “entities possessing a genome” also avoids requiring us to settle the separate and contested question of whether viruses should be called living.

This is another example of a mathematical model revealing its limits. The quantified statement may be true for the domain the classification was built to describe and false after that domain is enlarged.

TipExample: negating quantified statements

Negating a quantified statement also changes its quantifier:

\[ \neg(\forall x,\ P(x)) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists x\text{ such that }\neg P(x), \]

and

\[ \neg(\exists x,\ P(x)) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \forall x,\ \neg P(x). \]

In words, the negation of “every organism has property \(P\)” is not “no organism has property \(P\).” It is the weaker statement “at least one organism does not have property \(P\).”

Formal logic is not merely symbolic decoration. It structures reasoning, helps us evaluate arguments, and allows explicitly stated rules to be processed by computers. The following video illustrates that connection.

Formality in mathematical language

In ordinary conversation, context, tone, and shared experience often help us interpret an incomplete or ambiguous sentence. Mathematics aims to reduce that dependence on context. Formality means stating ideas in a sufficiently structured way that their objects, assumptions, and logical relationships can be interpreted consistently.

TipExample: formalising a classification rule

Consider the informal sentence:

Each animal has one classification.

It does not identify the animals or classifications, and “one” may mean “at least one” or “exactly one.” With \(A\), \(B'\), and \(f\) defined by our dietary model, the intended claim is

\[ \forall a\in A,\qquad \exists! b\in B'\text{ such that }f(a)=b. \]

This says that every animal receives exactly one classification. The words define the objects; the notation makes the logical structure visible.

TipMaking a statement sufficiently formal

Before presenting a mathematical claim, check that you have:

  1. identified the objects being discussed;
  2. defined the variables and their domains;
  3. stated any assumptions;
  4. used the required quantifiers and logical connectives; and
  5. made clear exactly what is being asserted.

The appropriate degree of formality depends on the purpose and audience. A classroom explanation may use more ordinary language than a formal proof, but neither should rely on avoidable ambiguity.

ImportantChapter summary

Sets identify the objects, relations connect them, functions assign outputs, binary operations combine objects, and logic governs the statements we make about them. Formality makes the objects, domains, assumptions, and conclusions explicit. Symbols are useful only when we understand what they represent and what they mean in the situation being studied.
