Mathematics in the Modern World

Bulacan State University, First Semester, A.Y. 2026-2027

This section contains lecture notes, examples, exercises, and/or supplementary materials for Mathematics in the Modern World.

NoteCurricular basis and intended audience

The scope and organisation of these lecture notes follow the framework for Mathematics in the Modern World set out by the Philippine Commission on Higher Education (CHED) in CHED Memorandum Order No. 20, series of 2013, General Education Curriculum: Holistic Understandings, Intellectual and Civic Competencies. CHED describes the course as addressing the nature of mathematics, its practical, intellectual, and aesthetic dimensions, and the application of mathematical tools in daily life.

The notes are written primarily for the BS Biology students whom I teach. Whenever practicable, the discussion uses examples drawn from biology, ecology, health, data analysis, and other situations that students may encounter in their studies and subsequent work. The course also considers less obvious applications: part of learning mathematics in the modern world is recognising its presence in problems and decisions that may not initially appear mathematical.

These independently prepared lecture notes are designed to support Mathematics in the Modern World and are aligned with the course framework established by CHED. They are personal teaching materials and should not be understood as an official publication of Bulacan State University or CHED.

House rules

  1. Printed notes are available if you need them. If you do not own, or do not have reliable access to, a laptop or mobile device, please approach me after class or during consultation hours. You do not need to explain your circumstances; I will provide printed copies of the relevant course notes free of charge.

  2. Attend regularly and arrive on time. Consistent attendance is expected because much of the learning will take place through explanation, discussion, and guided work in class. Attendance will contribute to the final mark in accordance with the course syllabus and university policy.

    The course notes provide a general guide to what we intend to discuss, not an inflexible transcript of every class meeting. Depending on the time available, the pace of the class, and the questions that arise, I may change the order of presentation, supplement the notes with another example, modify a discussion, or omit a portion that we cannot cover responsibly. It is therefore important to attend class so that you know what was actually covered and what emphasis was placed on it. Any change affecting examinable material will be announced clearly; you will not be assessed on a topic that was added without notice.

  3. Participate and ask questions. Questions are an important part of learning, and students are strongly encouraged to raise them in class. Consultation hours will be announced once the teaching timetable has been finalised. These meetings are suitable for questions requiring a longer discussion, including questions that go beyond the formal curriculum. This allows us to explore them properly while keeping class meetings focused on the material that everyone needs to learn. You may also send course-related questions or inquiries by email; the course email address is provided in the Logistics section above.

  4. Do not assume that biology contains little mathematics. Modern biology uses mathematics to describe population growth, inheritance, epidemics, ecological networks, experimental uncertainty, medical images, and the behaviour of complex biological systems. A particularly striking example is the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry: half recognised computational protein design, while the other half recognised protein-structure prediction—the work associated with AlphaFold2. Both achievements brought biological knowledge together with computation, statistics, and mathematical reasoning.

    NoteA case in point: mathematics and biology

    My own BSc Mathematics thesis is a case in point. Although it is a mathematics project, it is grounded in a biological and medical setting: photographs of the retina at the back of the eye. Such images are used when assessing conditions including diabetic retinopathy, in which diabetes damages blood vessels in the retina and can ultimately cause vision loss or blindness. I studied how computational topology can turn the connected components and loops appearing across a retinal image into numerical summaries, and whether those summaries remain reliable when the image is rotated, blurred, rescaled, or affected by noise. The project therefore brings together topology, computation, medical imaging, and the practical problem of retinal diagnosis. An expanded, self-study treatment is available in the Topological Data Analysis section, while the complete project repository is available at reyjosephnieto/ph. These links are included for curious readers; the mathematics required to study the project in detail lies well beyond the scope of this course.

    This course will not require students to train artificial neural networks. Its purpose is to develop the habits needed to recognise quantitative structure, interpret evidence, and reason carefully about unfamiliar problems. Those habits are useful in everyday decision-making and can be especially valuable to students who later pursue advanced study in biology, medicine, or related fields.

  5. Be present and considerate. I am not interested in teaching through fear; there is no educational value in intimidating students, and I am probably more approachable than you may initially think. In return, I expect everyone, as adults, to take responsibility for maintaining a respectful learning environment. Please do not hold side conversations while someone is speaking.

    Electronic devices should be put away during class unless I have asked you to use one for a learning activity or an approved accessibility need requires it. This is not about feeding the instructor’s ego. It is about giving yourself and those around you the attention needed to understand, question, and retain the material. I aim to choose topics that occasionally produce a genuine “Wow, talaga?” response; that moment of discovery is much harder to reach when our attention is divided between the lesson and a screen.

  6. Bring a notebook and a pen. Since electronic devices will ordinarily remain put away, come prepared to take handwritten notes. Avoid trying to record the lecture word for word. Instead, use brief bullet points to capture definitions, key ideas, examples, and questions in your own words. After class, close your notes and practise active recall: reconstruct the main ideas from memory, then reopen the notebook to identify what you missed or misunderstood. If you discover a gap, ask your classmates and compare notes; they are part of your learning community and may well become lifelong friends. If the point remains unclear, bring the question to me during consultation hours.

    NoteUse of generative AI

    I strongly encourage the cautious use of generative-AI tools—including ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek, and similar systems—for self-study. They can help you request another explanation, generate practice questions, or explore a topic from a different angle. However, they can also produce convincing but incorrect answers, so check their claims against the course notes and work through the mathematics yourself.

    Do not become dependent on these tools. They will not be available to help you in real time during graded assessments, where you must explain and apply ideas independently. All quizzes and recitations will be announced in advance and completed in class; there will be no take-home quizzes or recitations.

  7. Work steadily, but keep marks in perspective. I recognise that we are working within a tight academic schedule, so assessments will be designed deliberately and kept proportionate to the time and material available. Your marks will reflect the published assessment criteria and the work you submit.

    Examinations will not contain “curveball” questions based on material whose status has been left unclear. We may occasionally discuss an interesting idea beyond the essential course content; when we do, I will state explicitly whether it is examinable or non-examinable. This follows the transparent approach to assessment that I encountered in British universities. An examination may still ask you to apply a familiar idea in a new setting, but it will not quietly test a topic that was presented as optional enrichment.

    NoteA sidenote on our Lingua Franca

    You may also notice an occasional mixture of British and American English in these notes. My basic education used American English, whereas my university education used British English, and I still move between the two conventions. I apologise for any resulting inconsistency; it should never affect the mathematical meaning. Students may use either convention—the important things are clarity and consistency.

    I encourage everyone to practise using English because it is the principal language of much of the biology literature you will encounter. This is an encouragement, not a barrier to participation: English, Tagalog, and Cebuano are all welcome in class, and no one will be treated differently for choosing among them. I am open to questions in other languages as well, although I may need you or a classmate to translate if I do not understand them. If you find my accent or English difficult to follow, please tell me, either during class or privately. I can explain in Taglish whenever practicable while retaining the technical terms needed for the subject.

    Rather than worrying constantly about individual scores, focus on sustained effort, academic discipline, and responding constructively to feedback. If you do those things consistently, there should be no unpleasant surprises when you receive your class card. You have earned your place among Bulacan’s capable and hardworking students. Have faith in your ability—I have faith in you, too.

  8. Read the course notes before class. Since this is my first time teaching this course, the complete collection of lecture notes is still under construction. However, I will make the notes for each day’s scheduled topics available at least two weeks before we cover them.

    You are not expected to master the material before the lecture. The aim is simply to make an initial acquaintance with it: skim the headings, notice the main definitions and examples, and identify anything that already raises a question. Even a brief preview will make the topic feel less unfamiliar and help you follow the discussion without feeling lost.

    Much of our fear of mathematics is learned through discouraging experiences and the widespread assumption that only a few people can understand it. Mathematics can certainly be difficult, but it is not uniquely so: biology, economics, chemistry, and every other serious discipline present their own unfamiliar ideas and ways of thinking. As the course progresses, I hope you will discover that mathematics becomes approachable when it is explained patiently, connected to meaningful problems, and studied consistently.

  9. Correct mistakes—respectfully. I strongly encourage you to point out errors, whether they appear in the notes, in a classmate’s reasoning, or in something I have said or written. Corrections should address the idea rather than embarrass the person, and this is especially important when correcting one another.

    If the mistake is mine, please do not hesitate to say so. Valid corrections of mathematical or factual errors in my lectures or course materials may earn extra credit, under the same criteria and opportunity available to everyone. I am thoroughly egalitarian about this: finding something I have got wrong is not a challenge to my authority. My reaction will be unabated delight, because careful criticism improves both the notes and the class.

  10. Ask for help when you are struggling. You will not be judged for finding something difficult or for admitting that you do not yet understand it. I struggled in several classes when I was a student, too, and I made progress by seeking guidance rather than insisting that I solve every difficulty alone. You are part of a wider BulSU community that can support your learning. Your teacher should be one of the first people you can approach, and in this course, that person is me.
