Chapter 3: Problem Solving and Reasoning

From Observations to Defensible Conclusions

Mathematical models are precise relative to their assumptions, but they remain selective accounts of real phenomena. A model omits detail so that attention can be directed to the features relevant to a particular question.

Applying mathematics therefore requires more than a correct calculation. We must decide what to measure, which assumptions are reasonable, which methods suit the problem, and what the result does—and does not—justify.

NoteLearning outcomes

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:

  • distinguish inductive reasoning from deductive reasoning;
  • use observations to form a reasonable conjecture without treating the conjecture as certain;
  • explain how a deductive conclusion depends on its premises;
  • distinguish intuition and evidence from mathematical proof;
  • follow and explain a short mathematical proof; and
  • identify assumptions and limitations in a simple line of reasoning;
  • apply Pólya’s four steps to an accessible mathematical problem; and
  • organise and communicate a solution so that another reader can follow the method used.

Inductive and deductive reasoning

Reasoning allows us to move from information we already possess to a new conclusion. Two especially important forms are inductive and deductive reasoning. They serve different purposes and provide different degrees of certainty.

NoteDefinition: inductive reasoning

Inductive reasoning uses particular observations or examples to form a broader generalisation or conjecture.

Suppose a biologist observes that a population of bacteria increased under the same treatment in several experiments. The observations provide a reason to conjecture that the treatment promotes growth. That conclusion may be well-supported, but it is not guaranteed: a larger experiment, a different species, or a previously uncontrolled variable could produce another result.

Inductive reasoning is therefore valuable for discovering patterns and forming hypotheses. Its conclusions vary in strength according to the quality, quantity, and representativeness of the evidence.

NoteDefinition: deductive reasoning

Deductive reasoning begins with one or more premises and applies valid rules of inference to reach a conclusion that must be true whenever the premises are true.

Consider the following argument:

  1. All mammals are vertebrates.
  2. A dolphin is a mammal.
  3. Therefore, a dolphin is a vertebrate.

If the two premises are true and the form of the argument is valid, the conclusion cannot be false. Deduction does not make its premises true; it shows what necessarily follows if we accept them.

TipInduction and deduction compared
Feature Inductive reasoning Deductive reasoning
Typical direction particular observations to a general claim premises to a necessary consequence
Common purpose discover patterns and propose hypotheses establish what follows from assumptions
Status of conclusion plausible or probable certain, provided the premises are true and the reasoning is valid
Main vulnerability limited or unrepresentative evidence false premises or an invalid inference

The two forms of reasoning often work together. We may first notice a pattern inductively and state it as a conjecture. We then try to test, refine, or prove that conjecture. In an applied problem, observations may suggest a model, while deduction tells us what the model predicts.

Abductive reasoning — non-examinable

Abductive reasoning seeks a plausible explanation for the information available. For example, if several leaves on a plant suddenly turn yellow, one might propose nutrient deficiency as the best initial explanation.

Abduction and induction are related but not identical. Induction uses a collection of observations to support a broader pattern. Abduction starts from an observation, sometimes only a small set of observations, and asks which explanation would make it intelligible. An abductive conclusion is a candidate explanation, not a certainty; competing explanations must still be investigated.

Intuition, proof, and certainty

Reasoning often begins with intuition. We recognise patterns, anticipate answers, and develop a sense of what ought to be true before we can fully justify it.

NoteDefinition: mathematical intuition

Mathematical intuition is an informed expectation about a mathematical idea or result, developed through experience with patterns, examples, diagrams, and related concepts.

For example, the sequence

\[ 1,2,3,4,\ldots \]

suggests that adding \(1\) produces the next counting number. In a formal construction of the natural numbers, this successor relationship is built into their definition. Our ability to recognise and extend the written pattern, however, is intuitive.

Intuition is indispensable: it helps us decide what to investigate. It is not, by itself, a guarantee of truth. Some correct mathematical results are surprising, while some convincing patterns eventually fail. We therefore need a way to decide when an intuitive claim follows from our assumptions.

NoteDefinition: mathematical proof

A mathematical proof is a logically valid argument that establishes a statement from clearly stated definitions, assumptions, axioms, and results already known to be true.

Mathematical certainty is consequently conditional on the framework being used. Once the assumptions are fixed and the reasoning is valid, the conclusion follows necessarily. This is stronger than the way the word “proof” is often used in everyday conversation.

Evidence is not the same as proof

Evidence supports a conclusion. A proof establishes that the conclusion must follow. Checking one thousand examples may give excellent evidence for a general claim, but it does not ordinarily prove a claim about infinitely many cases. A single counterexample, on the other hand, is enough to disprove a universal claim.

You have already written simple proofs, even if they were not called proofs. When a solution to a calculation shows each step and justifies the operations used, it demonstrates that the answer follows from the given information. Proofs in more advanced mathematics follow the same basic principle, although their arguments may be less computational and require more careful use of definitions.

A recreational problem: is 0.999… less than 1?

Consider the finite decimals

\[ 0.9<1,\qquad 0.99<1,\qquad 0.999<1,\qquad 0.9999<1. \]

Every decimal displayed above terminates, and each is less than \(1\). It is tempting to extend this pattern to

\[ 0.\overline{9}=0.9999\ldots, \]

where the bar and ellipsis mean that the digit \(9\) repeats without end. Should this non-terminating decimal be less than, greater than, or equal to \(1\)?

The finite examples provide a natural intuition, but they do not settle the question. The infinite decimal is not the same object as any finite truncation such as \(0.9999\).

This is a recreational mathematical problem: a short, intriguing question whose purpose is to encourage exploration and careful reasoning. Such problems can be mathematically serious even when they are posed as puzzles.

TipClaim

The non-terminating decimal \(0.\overline{9}\) is equal to \(1\).

NoteProof

Let

\[ x=0.\overline{9}=0.9999\ldots. \]

Multiplying both sides by \(10\) shifts the decimal point one place to the right:

\[ 10x=9.9999\ldots=9+x. \]

Therefore,

\[ 10x=9+x, \]

so

\[ 9x=9 \]

and hence \(x=1\). Since \(x=0.\overline{9}\), we conclude that

\[ \boxed{0.\overline{9}=1}. \]

\(\square\)

A second proof using one-third — optional enrichment

We know from ordinary division that

\[ \frac{1}{3}=0.\overline{3}=0.3333\ldots. \]

Multiplying both sides by \(3\) gives

\[ 3\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)=3\left(0.\overline{3}\right). \]

The left-hand side is \(1\), while multiplying every digit on the right-hand side by \(3\) gives \(0.\overline{9}\). Therefore,

\[ \boxed{0.\overline{9}=1}. \]

\(\square\)

This second argument reaches the same conclusion through the familiar decimal expansion of \(\frac13\). A mathematical claim can have several valid proofs. They do not make the claim “more true,” but different proofs can reveal different connections and may vary in accessibility, generality, or usefulness.

This does not say that a finite decimal such as \(0.9999\) equals \(1\). Rather, the infinitely repeating decimal and \(1\) are two representations of the same real number. The example shows why a pattern suggested by finite cases must be handled carefully when we pass to an infinite process.

An alternative contradiction argument — non-examinable

Suppose, for contradiction, that

\[ 0.\overline{9}<1. \]

Then the difference

\[ y=1-0.\overline{9} \]

would be a positive real number. A basic property of the real numbers tells us that, for every positive \(y\), we can choose a positive integer \(n\) for which

\[ 10^{-n}<y. \]

The \(n\)-digit truncation containing \(n\) nines is \(1-10^{-n}\). Because \(0.\overline{9}\) contains still more nines, it is greater than that truncation. Thus

\[ 0<1-0.\overline{9}<10^{-n}. \]

Thus \(0<y<10^{-n}\), contradicting our choice \(10^{-n}<y\). There can therefore be no positive gap between \(0.\overline{9}\) and \(1\).

The trichotomy principle — non-examinable

For any two real numbers \(a\) and \(b\), exactly one of the following is true:

\[ a<b,\qquad a=b,\qquad\text{or}\qquad a>b. \]

This is the trichotomy principle. Consequently, if we establish both that \(0.\overline{9}\) is not less than \(1\) and that it is not greater than \(1\), equality is the only remaining possibility. The direct algebraic proof above is shorter, but trichotomy explains why ruling out the two inequalities would also settle the question.

What this example teaches us: Pólya’s four steps

The mathematician George Pólya organised problem solving into four broad steps. They are not a rigid recipe: a solver may move backwards and forwards between them. They nevertheless provide a useful structure for approaching an unfamiliar problem.

  1. Understand the problem. Identify what is given, what must be determined, and what the notation means. Here, we are comparing the non-terminating decimal \(0.\overline{9}\) with \(1\). The crucial distinction is that \(0.\overline{9}\) is not a finite decimal with an unusually large number of digits.

  2. Devise a plan. Connect the unfamiliar problem to something we already know. We can assign the repeating decimal to a variable and use multiplication by \(10\) to shift its decimal point. Alternatively, we can use the familiar identity \(\frac13=0.\overline{3}\). The strategy here is to reformulate the unfamiliar object in a form that ordinary algebra can handle.

  3. Carry out the plan. Perform each step carefully and justify it. Setting \(x=0.\overline{9}\) gives \(10x=9+x\), from which \(9x=9\) and therefore \(x=1\). The calculation establishes that \(0.\overline{9}=1\).

  4. Look back. Check whether the conclusion answers the original question and whether it is consistent with what we know. The result does not say that a finite decimal such as \(0.9999\) equals \(1\); it says that the infinitely repeating decimal and \(1\) are two representations of the same real number. We can also compare the first proof with the \(\frac13\) argument and ask whether either method suggests a more general principle.

Good mathematical reasoning does not require us to distrust intuition. It requires us to know what intuition can do: suggest a direction, not replace a justification.

A nontrivial example: deriving the quadratic formula

The same four steps apply to a more substantial problem. Suppose that

\[ ax^2+bx+c=0, \]

where \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) are real numbers and \(a\neq0\). We want to solve for \(x\) using only algebra.

A nontrivial proof: deriving the quadratic formula — guided enrichment

1. Understand the problem

The equation is quadratic because its highest power of \(x\) is \(2\). The condition \(a\neq0\) is essential: if \(a=0\), the term \(ax^2\) disappears and the equation is no longer quadratic.

Our goal is not to solve one particular equation. We want a formula that describes every possible solution in terms of the coefficients \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\).

2. Devise a plan

An equation of the form

\[ u^2=k \]

is easy to solve: \(u=\pm\sqrt{k}\). We therefore try to rewrite the left-hand side of the quadratic equation as a perfect square. This method is called completing the square.

3. Carry out the plan

NoteDerivation of the quadratic formula

Begin with

\[ ax^2+bx+c=0. \]

Because \(a\neq0\), we may divide every term by \(a\):

\[ x^2+\frac{b}{a}x+\frac{c}{a}=0. \]

Move the constant term to the right-hand side:

\[ x^2+\frac{b}{a}x=-\frac{c}{a}. \]

To complete the square, add

\[ \left(\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2=\frac{b^2}{4a^2} \]

to both sides:

\[ x^2+\frac{b}{a}x+\frac{b^2}{4a^2} = \frac{b^2}{4a^2}-\frac{c}{a}. \]

The left-hand side is now a perfect square. Combining the fractions on the right gives

\[ \left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2-4ac}{4a^2}. \]

Taking square roots gives

\[ x+\frac{b}{2a} = \pm\frac{\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2|a|}. \]

The symbol \(\pm\) accounts for both possible square roots. Because it already includes both signs, replacing \(|a|\) in the denominator by \(a\) does not change the two possible values. Finally, subtract \(\frac{b}{2a}\) from both sides:

\[ \boxed{x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}}. \]

This is the quadratic formula. \(\square\)

4. Look back

The quantity

\[ \Delta=b^2-4ac \]

is called the discriminant. For real-number solutions, it provides a quick check on the result:

  • if \(\Delta>0\), the formula gives two distinct real solutions;
  • if \(\Delta=0\), the two expressions coincide, giving one repeated real solution; and
  • if \(\Delta<0\), there is no real solution because the square root of a negative number is not real.

We can also substitute either resulting value of \(x\) into the original equation to verify it. Looking back also reveals why the formula has its familiar structure: it is not an arbitrary expression to be memorised, but the inevitable result of completing the square in the general quadratic equation.

TipWhy this counts as a proof

Each transformation preserves the solution set: we divide by a known nonzero number, add or subtract the same quantity on both sides, rewrite an expression as an equal perfect square, and account for both square roots using \(\pm\). The derivation therefore proves that every real solution of a quadratic equation with \(a\neq0\) has the form given by the quadratic formula.

A proof need not be this long. Once a result has been proved, we may use it in later arguments without deriving it again from the beginning. This often produces proofs that are both short and elegant. Brevity does not make a proof less rigorous, provided that every step is justified by a definition, assumption, or previously established result.

TipA one-line proof

Claim. The equation

\[ x^2+x+1=0 \]

has no real-number solutions.

Proof. Here, \(a=1\), \(b=1\), and \(c=1\), so \(b^2-4ac=1^2-4(1)(1)=-3\); therefore, in the quadratic formula, the radical \(\sqrt{-3}\) is imaginary, so the equation has no real solutions. \(\square\)

The proof is only one line because the substantial work has already been done in deriving the quadratic formula and interpreting its discriminant. This is how mathematics develops: established results become tools for proving new claims more efficiently.

Formulas are not magic

The quadratic formula may have been presented to you initially as something to memorise. As we have now seen, it has a proof: it follows from completing the square in the general quadratic equation. That derivation requires enough algebraic experience to follow several symbolic steps, so it is understandable that younger students are sometimes taught how to use the formula before they are shown fully why it works.

This does not mean that the formula appeared by magic. Mathematical formulas summarise relationships that can be justified. Some justifications are technical; others become clear once we arrange familiar objects in the right way.

An arithmetic sequence and its sum

An arithmetic sequence is a list of numbers in which the difference between consecutive terms is constant. For example,

\[ 2,\ 5,\ 8,\ 11,\ 14 \]

is an arithmetic sequence with first term \(2\) and common difference \(3\). When we add the terms of a sequence, the resulting sum is called a series:

\[ 2+5+8+11+14. \]

Suppose an arithmetic sequence has \(n\) terms. Let \(t_1\) denote its first term, \(d\) its common difference, and \(t_n\) its \(n\)th and final term. Then

\[ t_n=t_1+(n-1)d. \]

Its associated arithmetic series is

\[ S=t_1+(t_1+d)+(t_1+2d)+\cdots+t_n. \]

NoteDeriving the sum of an arithmetic series

Write the same sum twice: first in its original order and then in reverse.

\[ \begin{aligned} S&=t_1+(t_1+d)+(t_1+2d)+\cdots+t_n,\\ S&=t_n+(t_n-d)+(t_n-2d)+\cdots+t_1. \end{aligned} \]

Add the two equations term by term. The first terms give \(t_1+t_n\), the second terms also give \(t_1+t_n\), and the same is true for every remaining pair. Because there are \(n\) pairs,

\[ 2S=n(t_1+t_n). \]

Dividing by \(2\) gives

\[ \boxed{S=\frac{n}{2}(t_1+t_n)}. \]

Since \(t_n=t_1+(n-1)d\), the same result may also be written as

\[ \boxed{S=\frac{n}{2}\left[2t_1+(n-1)d\right]}. \]

\(\square\)

The problem-solving strategy is to change the representation: reversing the order of the terms exposes equal pairs that were not immediately visible in the original sum.

For the sequence \(2,5,8,11,14\), we have \(n=5\), \(t_1=2\), and \(t_n=14\). Therefore,

\[ S=\frac{5}{2}(2+14)=40, \]

which agrees with direct addition. The formula is a compact record of the pairing pattern revealed by writing the sum in both directions.

Then why do we memorise formulas?

There are several practical reasons.

  1. To manage the learner’s cognitive load. A learner must sometimes use a result before having the mathematical background needed to prove it. A ten-year-old child, for example, cannot reasonably be expected to reproduce a full formal derivation whenever a formula is used.

  2. To save time. Deriving every result from first principles whenever we need it would be tedious and inefficient. Once a formula has been established, remembering it allows us to concentrate on the new problem.

  3. To make implementation easier. Computers need explicit instructions expressed through arithmetic and logical operations. A formula packages a mathematical relationship into a form that can often be translated directly into code.

Memorisation is therefore useful, but it should not be mistaken for understanding. Ideally, we should know what a formula means, when its assumptions apply, and where to find or reconstruct its justification—even when we do not reproduce the proof every time we use it.

Mathematical patterns

Patterns often provide the starting point for mathematical discovery. We examine several cases, notice what they have in common, and use inductive reasoning to propose a general claim. We must then determine whether the pattern always holds.

Consider the following sums:

\[ \begin{aligned} 1+2&=3,\\ 3+8&=11,\\ 7+12&=19,\\ 15+24&=39. \end{aligned} \]

In every case, we add an odd integer to an even integer and obtain an odd integer. These observations suggest the following conjecture.

TipConjecture

The sum of an odd integer and an even integer is always odd.

The examples provide evidence, but they cannot cover every possible pair of integers. To prove the conjecture, we use the definitions of odd and even integers.

NoteDefinitions: even and odd integers

An integer is even if it can be written as \(2n\) for some integer \(n\). An integer is odd if it can be written as \(2m+1\) for some integer \(m\).

NoteProof

Let \(2m+1\) be any odd integer and let \(2n\) be any even integer, where \(m\) and \(n\) are integers. Their sum is

\[ (2m+1)+2n=2m+2n+1=2(m+n)+1. \]

Because the sum of two integers is an integer, \(m+n\) is an integer. The expression \(2(m+n)+1\) therefore has the form \(2k+1\) for an integer \(k=m+n\). By definition, it is odd. Hence, the sum of any odd integer and any even integer is odd. \(\square\)

The examples helped us discover the claim; the proof establishes it for all integers, including cases we did not test. This is a common mathematical workflow:

\[ \text{examples}\longrightarrow\text{pattern}\longrightarrow \text{conjecture}\longrightarrow\text{proof}. \]

Inductive reasoning versus mathematical induction — beyond the scope of this course

Here, inductive reasoning means drawing a general conjecture from particular examples. It should not be confused with mathematical induction, which is a specialised method for proving statements indexed by the natural numbers. Mathematical induction is beyond the scope of this course.

The (usually) easiest disproof: find a counterexample

Suppose someone makes the following claim:

TipClaim

Every prime number is odd.

The numbers \(3,5,7,11,\) and \(13\) support the claim, but checking more odd primes will never prove that every prime is odd. Instead, consider the number \(2\). It is prime and it is even.

NoteDisproof by counterexample

The number \(2\) satisfies the conditions of the claim—it is a prime number—but not its conclusion—it is not odd. Therefore, the claim that every prime number is odd is false. \(\square\)

The number \(2\) is called a counterexample. A counterexample is a particular case for which a general claim fails. To disprove a universal statement of the form “every object of this kind has this property,” one valid counterexample is enough.

This creates an important asymmetry:

  • many confirming examples may suggest a universal claim but do not prove it;
  • one counterexample disproves a universal claim immediately.

Finding a counterexample is therefore often the quickest way to settle a claim that is false. It is more accurate to call this a disproof rather than a proof of the original claim: what we have proved is that the claim is false.

Another disproof by counterexample — non-examinable

Consider the claim:

Every number with a non-terminating decimal representation is irrational.

The decimal

\[ 0.\overline{9}=0.9999\ldots \]

does not terminate. However, we proved earlier that

\[ 0.\overline{9}=1. \]

The number \(1\) is rational because it can be written as the ratio \(\frac11\) of two integers. Thus, \(0.\overline{9}\) has a non-terminating decimal representation but represents a rational number. It is therefore a counterexample, and the original claim is false. \(\square\)

The missing distinction is repetition. Non-terminating repeating decimals are rational, whereas the decimal expansion of an irrational number is non-terminating and non-repeating.
